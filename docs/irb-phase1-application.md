Professor James Gaskin, a Professor of Information Systems at Brigham Young University, holds a Ph.D. in Information Systems and has been an academic leader with over 200 publications and more than 12,000 citations. His work focuses on understanding the relationship between digital behaviors and mental health, particularly in the contexts of stress, self-esteem, resilience, and well-being. One of his notable studies, "The Role of Stress and Motivation in Problematic Smartphone Use Among College Students" (co-authored with JL Wang, HZ Wang, and LH Wang, Computers in Human Behavior, 2015), investigates how stress and motivational factors contribute to problematic smartphone use--a behavior associated with anxiety and sleep disruptions. In addition, Gaskin's research on the impact of social networking site use on friendship quality and well-being, as seen in "The Effects of Social Networking Site (SNS) Use on College Students' Friendship and Well-Being" (co-authored with JL Wang, LA Jackson, and HZ Wang, Computers in Human Behavior, 2014), explores how digital engagement shapes social relationships and mental health.

Gaskin also explores the mechanisms through which SNS usage impacts psychological outcomes, as seen in "The Mediating Roles of Upward Social Comparison and Self-Esteem in the Association Between Social Networking Site Usage and Psychological Outcomes" (co-authored with JL Wang, HZ Wang, and S Hawk, Frontiers of Psychology, 2017). This work examines how self-esteem and social comparison mediate the relationship between SNS use and mental health. Additionally, his article "Psychological Resilience Can Help Combat the Effect of Stress on Problematic Social Networking Site Usage" (co-authored with XL Hou, HZ Wang, C Guo, and DH Rost, Personality and Individual Differences, 2017) highlights the role of resilience in mitigating the stress associated with digital behavior.

Gaskin recognizes the critical role that technology--whether AI or social media--plays in modern life and its profound implications for mental well-being. His research on digital behaviors illustrates an understanding of how technology can influence mental health. These contributions provide valuable insights not only for academic research but also for practical applications in organizational and therapeutic settings.

In terms of methodology, he has undertaken numerous studies using experimental, survey, observational, and interview approaches.

Lay Summary
Provide a summary of the valid research hypothesis and/or appropriate objectives/research questions to support this project.
Nearly 1 billion people globally are affected by mental health disorders, yet access to therapy remains limited due to high costs, therapist shortages, cultural stigma, and language barriers. AI-based mental health tools offer 24/7 support, affordability, and multilingual capabilities, but they often lack the emotional sensitivity, ethical discernment, and cultural awareness that human therapists provide. At the same time, human therapy is constrained by fatigue, limited availability, and high costs. These limitations suggest an urgent need for new models of care that combine the strengths of both AI systems and human clinicians.

To address these challenges, we propose a hybrid therapy model that integrates AI tools with human therapist involvement. This IRB, however, focuses only on the information-gathering and initial impressions stages of the model. While licensed clinicians will be interviewed to provide expert perspective and feedback, they will not oversee or engage in participants’ interactions with the AI system during this study. In the broader model, AI assists with tasks such as mood tracking, intake assessments, and between-session support, while human therapists provide relational depth, ethical oversight, and clinical expertise. This study will explore whether such an approach can improve therapeutic engagement, increase access to support, and reduce the workload on mental health providers.

Clear, logical, and sufficient support from the scientific literature (e.g., pilot data and citations from current literature) to justify the conduct of the study

Nearly 1 billion people globally are affected by mental health disorders, yet access to therapy remains limited due to high costs, therapist shortages, cultural stigma, and language barriers (Kuhail et al., 2024). AI-based platforms—such as chatbots and voice assistants—offer 24/7 support, affordability, and multilingual capabilities. However, these systems often lack the emotional sensitivity, ethical discernment, and cultural awareness that human therapists provide (Ahmadi & Hammond, 2023; Bhatt, 2024). At the same time, human therapy is constrained by fatigue, limited availability, and high costs (Du et al., 2024; Fiske et al., 2018). Research suggests that hybrid models that combine AI tools with therapist oversight can preserve clinical quality while increasing efficiency and access (Prescott et al., 2023; Yonatan-Leus & Brukner, 2024).
The definition of objectives and/or endpoints
This study aims to explore perceptions of a proposed hybrid AI-supported therapy model and to assess whether AI-assisted tools may support therapeutic engagement and access while potentially alleviating aspects of clinician workload. Participants—BYU undergraduates aged 18 or older and BYU-affiliated licensed therapists—will complete surveys about their mental health experiences and attitudes toward AI-assisted support, and some undergraduate participants may be invited to interact with an AI chatbot for information-gathering purposes. Licensed therapists will participate in interviews to share professional perspectives on how AI tools might assist in clinical practice, without overseeing or engaging in participant interactions. Data from this exploratory phase will inform the design of future study phases, which will be submitted for review under a separate IRB.

Validation and reliability are established through a multi-layered anonymization process designed to ensure consistent, repeatable removal of personally identifiable information (PII) and protected health information (PHI) prior to research use. All AI conversation logs undergo a dual-pass automated redaction process using a model explicitly instructed to comply with the HIPAA Safe Harbor de-identification standard, targeting all 18 identifier categories. The second pass functions as a verification step to identify edge cases, contextual identifiers, or formatting anomalies that may not be captured in a single pass, increasing reliability across varied message structures and content types .

Reliability is further validated through mandatory human verification. Trained Research Assistants review all redacted content before inclusion in any research dataset and manually remove any residual identifiers if detected. Importantly, reviewers never access original, unredacted messages, ensuring consistent enforcement of anonymity while preventing re-exposure of sensitive data. All corrections are logged, providing an auditable trail that supports procedural validation and oversight .

System reliability is reinforced by architectural safeguards, including strict data separation between original clinical content and redacted research data, as well as automated deletion of original content after a short retention period (default 24 hours). This ensures that anonymity is maintained over time and that identifiable data is not retained beyond clinical necessity. Collectively, these technical and procedural controls validate that anonymization is consistently achieved and reliably maintained across all AI conversation logs used for research purposes .

What are you asking subjects to do?
All participants will complete an online eligibility screening and provide electronic informed consent prior to participation. This applies to both student and therapist participants.

Part 1

Student participants will complete an online survey administered through Qualtrics that collects demographic information, prior therapy experience, and perceptions of AI use in therapy contexts (Part 1 Survey).

Part 2

Therapist participants will complete a one-on-one Zoom interview focused on their experiences and perspectives regarding AI in therapy. All therapists will complete an interview using a semi-structured interview guide, which has been uploaded as part of the IRB submission.

Part 3
Another set of student participants will be invited to participate. Participation in this part of the study is limited by the number of available session slots (50 - 60 slots).

Part 3 consists of a single, one-time interaction with a multilingual, voice-enabled AI chatbot developed by the research team using OpenAI’s Whisper AI and GPT-4o models. The interaction will take place in the SONA lab on campus. The chatbot is configured to simulate therapeutic-style conversations using a standardized system prompt.

The Part 3 session lasts approximately one hour and includes a brief pre-interaction survey (Part 1 Survey that Part 1 students also complete), one interaction with the AI chatbot, and a short post-interaction survey (Part 3 Survey).

*Important Note* -- During the SONA lab portion of the study (Part 3), CAPS therapists will not be "live" or physically present in the lab. However, CAPS will serve as the designated clinical support resource in an event where a participant experiences a crisis or if a potential mental health concern is identified by a researcher or flagged by the AI therapist assistant system (see more information on this below).

Safety and Confidentiality

This study does not provide therapy, diagnose conditions, or offer individualized clinical advice. If a participant expresses distress or indicates potential risk to safety, the AI system is designed to encourage use of appropriate support resources, including the BYU CAPS crisis line and the national 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

We are partnering with BYU CAPS professionals throughout the study. As mentioned above, CAPS will serve as the designated clinical support resource in an event where a participant experiences a crisis or if a potential mental health concern is identified by a researcher or flagged by the AI therapist assistant system.

If a concern arises, established escalation procedures will be followed. Participants will remain unidentified to the research team, and a separate, trained research assistant will escort the participant to the CAPS office for professional support. This process ensures timely intervention while maintaining participant confidentiality and minimizing risk.

Any information that could contain Protected Health Information (PHI) is automatically de-identified using the HIPAA Safe Harbor method prior to storage. Data are processed through a zero-retention endpoint, and the research team does not have access to unredacted personal information. A signed Business Associate Agreement (BAA) is in place with the model provider (OpenAI). No clinical records are collected as part of this study. As a failsafe for this process, we will have a designated Research Assistant assigned to verify the effectiveness of the redaction model.

When will participants do the procedures?

All study activities take place at times convenient for participants. Electronic consent and student surveys are completed online at the participant’s discretion. Therapist interviews are conducted via scheduled Zoom calls. The single AI interaction session takes place in the SONA lab on campus and is immediately followed by a post-interaction survey and, if applicable, a brief interview.

In our current system architecture, the system prompt informs the actions that the voice model takes. When the system internally flags something, it informs the response the model gives. Specifically, crisis detection and flagging are performed through a separate post-processing module applied to redacted transcripts. This module uses keyword detection, sentiment scoring, and contextual trajectory analysis to assign a structured crisis risk score and severity level. This occurs within the research system and does not modify vendor model behavior or training.
